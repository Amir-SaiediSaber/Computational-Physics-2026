\section{Head-to-head comparison under the shared protocol}
\label{sec:comparison}

This section contains the central results of the paper: all four methods, reduced to per-class binary masks, scored with identical metrics on the identical spatially disjoint validation tiles (Sect.~\ref{sec:metrics_protocol}). The comparison is organised per class, because the two classes with usable supervision pose opposite problems: \texttt{impact\_crater} is nearly saturated (prevalence 0.82) while \texttt{wrinkle\_ridge} is sparse (prevalence 0.0064) but genuinely learnable.

\subsection{Impact crater: the base-rate regime}
\label{sec:comp_crater}

\paragraph{The base-rate trap.}
On the full spatial validation set, the crater channel covers 82\% of pixels and a \emph{predict-everything} baseline attains F1 $= 0.90$ and IoU $= 0.82$ with MCC $= 0$. Table~\ref{tab:crater_allval} shows that the SmallUNet essentially reproduces this baseline: F1 $= 0.90$, MCC $= 0.01$. Its high F1 is not evidence of skill --- the network has learned to output the base rate, and on this channel that is very hard to beat, since half of the tiles contain no negative pixels at all. Any comparison that ranked methods by F1 or IoU here would be ranking base-rate reproduction; this is why MCC is the primary metric of the protocol.

\paragraph{Regime decomposition.}
The aggregate numbers hide a regime structure. Partitioning the validation tiles by crater coverage (Table~\ref{tab:crater_regime}, Fig.~\ref{fig:regime}) shows that the best method \emph{flips} with the regime. On crater-sparse tiles (1--20\% coverage), where craters are countable objects, the instance and detection formulations lead: Mask R-CNN (MCC 0.19) ahead of YOLOv8 (0.16), with the classical baseline (0.07) modest and the SmallUNet at chance (MCC $\approx 0$). On crater-dense tiles ($\geq$20\% coverage), where the channel is a coverage layer, YOLOv8 keeps a positive signal (0.24) while Mask R-CNN collapses ($-0.11$; such tiles are out of distribution for its instance-target preparation, which skips channels above 20\% coverage, Sect.~\ref{sec:maskrcnn}), and the SmallUNet remains at chance in \emph{both} regimes --- its seemingly strong aggregate F1 is entirely base rate. One caveat applies: MCC and F1 at a fixed operating point are sensitive to the calibrated threshold, so the absolute values shift with $\tau$; the rankings and the regime flip are stable across the calibration range we scanned.

\subsection{Wrinkle ridge: the class with genuine signal}
\label{sec:comp_ridge}

Wrinkle ridges are the mirror image of craters: prevalence 0.0064, so chance-level performance is near zero and any positive score reflects learned structure. The comparison runs on the 524 ridge-bearing tiles of the spatial validation split (210 calibration / 314 test). The SmallUNet entry is the definitive no-augmentation spatial checkpoint of Sect.~\ref{sec:mr_augmentation}; the YOLOv8 entry is the committed ridge-focused six-class model (the ensemble study of Sect.~\ref{sec:split_study} retrains a dedicated single-class ridge detector under both split protocols); the classical entry is the Sato ridge filter of Sect.~\ref{sec:classical}.

The ranking reverses relative to the sparse-crater regime: \textbf{SmallUNet (MCC 0.452) $>$ Mask R-CNN (0.366) $\gg$ YOLOv8 (0.076) $>$ Sato ($\approx$ chance)}. The two pixel-supervised models are far ahead, which is consistent with the nature of the class: ridges are thin, extended, and connected --- pixel supervision describes them well, whereas bounding boxes are a poor geometric fit (a diagonal ridge fills a small fraction of its own box). The SmallUNet additionally beats Mask R-CNN at $\sim$1/60th of the inference cost. The classical filter, which cannot use context, fires on all elongated bright structures and stays at chance. Together with Sect.~\ref{sec:comp_crater} this yields the central per-class conclusion: \emph{semantic segmentation wins where dense pixel supervision exists; instance detection wins for sparse countable objects; nothing wins on a saturated channel.}

\subsection{Effect of the split protocol: a controlled study}
\label{sec:split_study}

The historical single-method experiments in this project used random tile splits (Sect.~\ref{sec:splits}); the comparison above uses the spatial split. To quantify what that choice does to a reported number --- and to make the two protocols commensurable --- we retrained \emph{identical} single-class YOLOv8n detectors (pretrained \texttt{yolov8n}, image size 256, batch 16, 40 epochs, identical seeds and hyperparameters) on datasets built by the same box-extraction code, changing \emph{only} the split: a random 80/20 tile split versus the spatial block split. Both arms of each pair see comparable training volumes (crater: 5\,659 vs.\ 5\,077 training tiles; ridge: 2\,339 vs.\ 2\,202). We report each model on its own validation set, plus a decomposition row: the random-split model evaluated on the \emph{spatial} validation tiles, which separates the training-side from the evaluation-side component of the leakage (note that this is \emph{not} unseen terrain for the random-split model, whose training tiles span the whole region --- that is precisely what the row measures).

Both classes replicate the effect, with strongly class-dependent size (Table~\ref{tab:split_study}). For wrinkle ridges the protocol choice alone nearly doubles the reported score: mAP50 0.146 under the random split against 0.077 under the spatial split, for an identical model, budget, and data pipeline. The decomposition row separates the two contributions. Compared on \emph{identical} validation tiles, the random-trained model outscores the spatial-trained one (0.117 vs.\ 0.077): the only difference between the two is that the random model's training set contains tiles overlapping the validation blocks, so this $+0.040$ is a training-side contamination benefit, not an ability difference. The remaining $+0.029$ (0.146 vs.\ 0.117, same model, different validation tiles) is the evaluation-side component: the random protocol's own validation tiles share half their pixels with training tiles. For impact craters the total effect at this budget is much smaller (0.122 vs.\ 0.106): the near-saturated crater channel offers little memorisable local detail that is not already ubiquitous, so proximity to training tiles buys less.

The budget dependence is confirmed directly by extending the ridge pair to 150 epochs (Fig.~\ref{fig:split_divergence}): the random-split validation curve climbs monotonically throughout (0.146 at epoch 40, 0.217 at 150, still rising), while the spatial-split curve saturates before epoch 40 and never leaves the 0.06--0.08 band. The gap therefore \emph{grows} with training --- from $\times 1.9$ at 40 epochs to $\times 2.6$ at 150 --- because every additional epoch lets the model memorise more of the near-duplicate content shared between the random protocol's train and validation tiles, while genuine generalisation stopped improving long before. At epoch 150 the random-trained model also scores 0.225 on the spatial validation tiles against the spatial-trained model's 0.082 on those \emph{same tiles}: at high budget, proximity of the training set to the evaluation terrain is worth three times the score.

\begin{figure}[htbp!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth,keepaspectratio]{figures/comparison/fig_split_divergence.png}
\caption{Validation mAP50 of the single-class wrinkle-ridge detector over 150 training epochs, under the two split protocols (thin lines: per-epoch values; thick: 7-epoch rolling mean; dotted line: the matched-budget comparison of Table~\ref{tab:split_study}). Two practical consequences follow. First, scores reported under random tile splits on this dataset are not comparable to spatially split scores --- and the discrepancy is largest precisely for the classes where detection genuinely works, i.e.\ where there is signal to memorise. Development-time scores quoted at much larger training budgets under random-split protocols (Sect.~\ref{sec:yolo_ensemble}) therefore overstate generalisation increasingly with budget, as continued training rewards memorisation of near-duplicate samples that a spatial protocol excludes by construction (Fig.~\ref{fig:split_divergence}). Second, the honest absolute level matters for the comparison of Sect.~\ref{sec:comp_ridge}: the dedicated single-class ridge detector under the spatial protocol (mAP50 0.077) is fully consistent with the committed six-class model's shared-protocol showing (MCC 0.076), confirming that YOLO's weakness on ridges is a property of the box representation on curvilinear features, not an artefact of any particular training run.


\subsection{Trade-offs beyond accuracy}
\label{sec:tradeoffs}

Accuracy is one axis; Table~\ref{tab:ridge} also reports cost. Mask R-CNN pays $\sim$1.1\,s per tile on CPU --- a full south-pole pass (3\,639 tiles) takes over an hour, versus about a minute for the SmallUNet and YOLOv8 and seconds for the classical baseline. Data preparation effort also differs sharply: the segmentation networks consume the semantic masks directly, while both detection methods require a semantic-to-instance conversion whose noise (merged neighbouring objects, fragmented ridges) propagates into training targets (Sect.~\ref{sec:maskrcnn}). Interpretability runs in the opposite direction: every parameter of the classical baseline has physical meaning, detector boxes are auditable object by object, and dense probability maps require post-hoc thresholding whose calibration the protocol had to fix. None of these axes changes the per-class accuracy ranking, but they decide deployability: for survey-scale mapping of ridge-like features the SmallUNet offers the best accuracy-per-cost, while for counting sparse objects Mask R-CNN's lead comes at two orders of magnitude more compute.
